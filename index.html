zone 01 · epipelagic · 0 – 200 m

The Sunlight Zone

A layer thinner than Manhattan is long — yet it holds roughly 90% of all marine life. Everything below it lives in the dark, off whatever drifts down from here.

10 m

Pressure has already doubled

Ten metres of seawater weighs as much as the entire atmosphere above it. Your lungs are squeezed to half their surface volume — and we've barely started.

40 m

Recreational scuba stops here

Past this line, ordinary dive training and ordinary air are no longer enough. The nitrogen in every breath starts to behave like laughing gas.

100 m

One breath, one hundred metres

Elite freedivers reach this depth on a single lungful of air — a round trip of about four minutes. Sunlight here is already twenty times weaker than at the surface.

200 m

Photosynthesis ends

Around one percent of sunlight is left — too little for plants. The ocean's food factory shuts down at this line. Everything below is an economy of leftovers.

zone 02 · mesopelagic · 200 – 1,000 m

The Twilight Zone

A permanent blue dusk. Down here, about nine out of ten animals make their own light — to hunt, to hide, to talk. It is the largest light show on Earth, and almost nobody has seen it.

332 m

The deepest scuba dive in history

Ahmed Gabr, Red Sea, 2014. Going down took 12 minutes. Coming back up safely took almost 14 hours of decompression stops. No one has beaten it since.

535 m

Deepest dive by a bird

An emperor penguin holds the record — deeper than the Burj Khalifa is tall, in water at −1.8 °C, on one breath, in the dark of the Antarctic winter.

1 000 m

The last photon

This is as far as sunlight gets. Below this line the ocean has been completely dark for four billion years. From here on, every light you see is made by something alive.

zone 03 · bathypelagic · 1,000 – 4,000 m

The Midnight Zone

No light. Near-freezing water. Pressure that would crush a submarine from World War II like a can. And still: life, everywhere.

2 000 m

Sperm whale hunting grounds

Whales built like submarines free-dive to these depths and hunt giant squid by sound, in total darkness — the loudest animal on Earth echolocating through the black.

3 688 m

You just passed the average

This is the mean depth of the entire ocean. Statistically speaking, most of planet Earth looks like what's around you right now: cold, black, and utterly unexplored.

zone 04 · abyssopelagic · 4,000 – 6,000 m

The Abyss

The abyssal plains cover more than half of the Earth's surface — the most common landscape on the planet, and the least visited. We have better maps of Mars.

4 500 m

It is snowing. It never stops.

The flecks drifting past you are marine snow — the slow fallout of life above. A single flake can take weeks to reach the bottom. Down here, it is the entire food supply.

5 500 m

Two degrees above freezing

The temperature has barely changed for the last three thousand metres — and it won't again. The water around you has not touched sunlight for centuries: deep currents take up to a thousand years to cycle back to the surface.

6 000 m

The gate of the underworld

Below this line, the ocean floor tears open into trenches. Scientists named what comes next after Hades, god of the dead. Only a handful of machines ever built can survive past this point.

zone 05 · hadal · 6,000 – 10,935 m

The Hadal Zone

Narrow scars in the planet's crust, where one tectonic plate dives beneath another. The walls close in; the pressure keeps climbing. You are now deeper than 99.9% of the ocean floor.

8 849 m

Mount Everest, drowned

If you tore Everest from the Himalayas and dropped it into this trench, its summit would sit right here — still more than two kilometres of black water above it.

10 916 m

23 January 1960

The bathyscaphe Trieste — Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh, the first humans at the bottom of the Challenger Deep. Their reward for the five-hour fall: twenty minutes on the floor, peering through a single cracked window. No one returned for 52 years.

10 925 m

The new visitors

James Cameron, solo, 2012. Victor Vescovo, 2019 — whose submarine Limiting Factor made the trip repeatable for the first time. Still: more people have stood on the Moon than have reached the place you are about to arrive at.

challenger deep · mariana trench

10,935 m
The bottom.

The deepest point in the ocean. The pressure here is about 1,100 atmospheres — eight tonnes pressing on every square inch, the weight of a small car concentrated on your thumbnail.

Sea cucumbers, amphipods and microbial mats live here in the dark. And in 1998, a survey camera at the bottom of the trench photographed something else that had made the journey down: a plastic bag. Nowhere on Earth is far enough away from us. That is the best reason to take care of the surface.
